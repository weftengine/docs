    Exposure and tone mapping¶

    The renderer lights the scene in physical units, exposes it like a camera, and then tone maps the exposed image to the screen. This page describes the three steps, the settings that control them, and the units the lights are in. The code is in Source/Weft.Rendering (ExposureMath.cs, Gt7ToneMapping.cs and the shaders forward.slang, exposure.slang and tonemap.slang).

    lights (lux, candela)  →  forward pass: luminance in cd/m²  →  × exposure  →  HDR image (exposed values)
                                                                                    │
                                          luminance histogram → auto-exposure ──────┤  (next frame's exposure)
                                                                                    ▼
                                                          tone mapper (GT7 or PBR Neutral) → sRGB → screen

    Units: light is photometric¶

    The forward pass computes luminance in cd/m² (nits) from lights in the units the glTF format uses (KHR_lights_punctual):

    What Unit In the code
    The sun (Light.Sun, RenderScene.SunColor) lux (illuminance on a surface facing the sun) SunColor is color × lux; a surface facing the sun is lit with that many lux
    Point and spot lights (Light.Point, Intensity) candela (luminous intensity) color × candela, divided by the squared distance: lux on a surface facing the light
    Ambient (AmbientSky, AmbientGround) cd/m²: the radiance of the environment multiplied by the surface's albedo (not by π: a radiance is not an illuminance)
    Emissive (MaterialData.EmissiveFactor, KHR_materials_emissive_strength) cd/m² (nits) added as it is
    Unlit materials, the sky color, debug lines display-referred colors not light: the exposure does not change them

    A surface of albedo a lit by E lux straight on, seen from the front, has the luminance E · (a / π) (plus a little specular): 100 000 lux of sun on a white wall of 0.8 is about 25 000 cd/m². The tests check this: a sun of 2 lux and a point light of 200 cd 10 m above the same ground give the same picture, and its luminance is the one the BRDF gives.

    A point light's fall-off is the inverse square with a smooth window that reaches exactly zero at its radius (Range, or where it falls below 0.05 lux when it has none, at most 500 m). That radius is a culling bound and does not depend on the exposure, so a very dark scene, exposed for starlight, can show the edge of a light whose range is left to the default; give such lights an explicit Range.

    Content authored before this page (suns of 3, lamps of 80 candela) is far too dim to be real, which is why the default exposure is automatic: whatever the scale of the lights, the image is exposed for it. When you author in real units (a sunny day is about 100 000 lux, a lit room 100 to 500 lux, moonlight 0.1 lux) the exposure also tells you what a camera would see.

    Exposure¶

    A camera's exposure is one number, the EV100 (exposure value at ISO 100). For an aperture f/N, a shutter time t in seconds and a sensitivity S:

    EV100 = log2(N² / t) − log2(S / 100)

    One stop of aperture, shutter or ISO is one EV. A sunny day with f/16 at 1/100 s and ISO 100 is EV100 14.6; a lit room is about 5.

    The forward pass multiplies the luminance by 1 / (1.2 · 2^EV100) (the saturation-based exposure of a camera at that EV100) before it writes the HDR image. The HDR image therefore holds exposed values: 1.0 is the luminance that just saturates the sensor, a bright sun does not overflow the half-float image the way raw cd/m² would, and everything after the forward pass (tone mapping, the debug draw) works on numbers around 0 to 1. A half float tops out at 65 504, and a very bright highlight at a low EV100 can still reach it (at EV100 −6 the exposure is 53), so the forward pass clamps its result to 65 504 instead of letting it become an infinity, which would turn into NaN in later passes; the tone mapper also takes an infinity as 65 504.

    RenderSettings.ExposureMode chooses the EV100:

    Mode EV100 Settings
    Fixed the value you set Ev100
    Camera from the physical camera Aperture, ShutterSeconds, Iso
    Auto (default) measured from the scene every frame, adapted over time Ev100 (first frame), MinEv100, MaxEv100, AdaptationSpeedBright, AdaptationSpeedDark, AutoLowPercentile, AutoHighPercentile

    RenderSettings.Exposure is an exposure compensation as a factor (2 is one stop brighter) on top of any mode.

    var settings = new RenderSettings
{
    ExposureMode = ExposureMode.Camera,
    Aperture = 8f, ShutterSeconds = 1f / 125f, Iso = 100f,   // EV100 ≈ 13
    Exposure = 1.5f,                                          // and a little brighter
};

    Auto-exposure¶

    Two compute passes run after the forward pass, and nothing is read back to the CPU:

    1. Luminance histogram (buildHistogram). Every pixel where something lit was drawn adds one to a bin of a 256-bin histogram of its luminance's log2. Bin 0 counts what is not measured (black, below the range, NaN or infinite), bins 1 to 254 divide the range 2⁻¹⁰ to 2¹⁸ cd/m² (0.001 to 262 000, 28 stops, about a ninth of a stop per bin) evenly, and bin 255 holds what is at or above the top. A pixel's luminance is its value divided by the exposure the frame was drawn with. What is not light is left out: pixels where nothing was drawn (the sky color), found by their depth, and opaque unlit pixels, which the forward pass writes with alpha 0 (an unlit color divided by the exposure would read as a luminance that moves with the exposure, and push it further every frame). A blended unlit surface mixes with what is behind it and is measured with it. The group counts in shared memory and adds its counts to the global histogram.
    2. Resolve exposure (resolveExposure). One thread averages the histogram without its darkest 10 % and brightest 10 % of pixels (a few lamps should not darken the image, a dark corner should not brighten it), gets the EV100 log2(L · 100 / 12.5) that exposes that luminance as a mid-gray (12.5 is the reflected-light meter's calibration constant), clamps it to MinEv100 and MaxEv100, and moves the exposure towards it: ev += (target − ev) · (1 − e^(−speed · dt)), with AdaptationSpeedBright (default 3 per second) when the scene got brighter and AdaptationSpeedDark (1) when it got darker, as an eye does. It writes the exposure into a small persistent buffer and clears the histogram for the next frame.

    The forward pass of the next frame reads the exposure from that buffer, on the GPU, so no frame waits for the previous one. The consequences:

    • The first frame of auto-exposure, and the first after another mode, is drawn with Ev100; the histogram then sets the exposure to the target at once (no crawl from a stale value), and the second frame is right.
    • The frame time for the adaptation is measured between calls to Render (clamped to a quarter of a second); RenderSettings.DeltaTime sets it, which the tests use to be repeatable.
    • There is no way yet to snap the adaptation on a camera cut or a scene load while staying in Auto (it restarts only after another mode); after a cut the exposure takes a second or two to settle. Not done.
    • The state belongs to the SceneRenderer: two views drawn by one renderer share an exposure. Give each its own renderer when they should adapt separately.

    ExposureMath is the CPU twin of all of this (EV100, the scale, a bin, the percentile average, the adaptation), and the tests run the shader passes against it.

    Tone mapping¶

    RenderSettings.Tonemapper chooses what turns the exposed image into the display's range. The result is encoded with the sRGB transfer function in the shader (the swapchain is UNORM).

    Tonemapper.Gt7 (default)¶

    Gran Turismo 7's operator, from Polyphony Digital's sample implementation (gt7_tone_mapping.cpp, MIT licence; the notice is in Legal/THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.md), ported to tonemap.slang and, as its twin, to Gt7ToneMapping.cs. It works like this:

    1. The color goes to linear Rec.2020 (the renderer's working space is Rec.709, the sRGB primaries) and is scaled to the operator's frame-buffer units, where 1.0 is 100 cd/m².
    2. Each channel goes through a curve: a power-law toe, a linear section (the identity from 0.538 up to 0.444 of the peak) and an exponential shoulder that levels off just above the display's peak.
    3. The result is also taken to ICtCp (a perceptual color space with the lightness I apart from the chroma Ct and Cp). The curved color's lightness is combined with the input's chroma, which is faded out as the lightness nears the peak. Per-channel curves alone shift hues (saturated bright colors skew towards yellow or cyan); keeping the chroma fixes the hue, and fading it makes the brightest colors go to white.
    4. The two results are blended (60 % of the chroma-preserving one), cut to the peak, and scaled so that the peak is the display's white.

    SDR mode is the one the renderer uses: the display's white is the operator's paper white of 250 cd/m² (2.5 in frame-buffer units), which is where the curve's parameters were tuned. The renderer maps the exposed value 1.0 (the luminance that saturates the sensor) to that paper white, so a mid-gray at 18 % is shown as a mid-tone and the sensor's saturation just under white. The output is converted back to Rec.709 (colors outside the sRGB gamut are clipped) before the sRGB encoding.

    Two small differences from the original, both for the GPU: negative and NaN channels are taken as 0 and an infinity as 65 504 before the operator (it assumes valid input, and an infinity makes NaN in the ICtCp conversion), and the constants it sets up once for the SDR peak (the shoulder's kA, kB, kC and the lightness of the peak) are literals in the shader, which a test compares with the C# twin's. The C# twin clamps an HDR peak to the original's 250 to 10 000 cd/m². HDR output (the operator's HDR mode on an HDR10 swapchain) is not implemented. The twin is also tested against the original's own test inputs (SDR and HDR peaks of 1000, 4000 and 10 000), with the outputs computed from the original's source.

    Tonemapper.PbrNeutral¶

    The Khronos PBR Neutral tone mapper, as before. It leaves colors below a compression point alone, so a base color texture shows as it was authored, which suits product views and flat-lit stylized scenes. Its input is the exposed value as it is.

    Reading the settings together¶

    I want Use
    A scene lit in real units that adapts like an eye ExposureMode.Auto (the default), with MinEv100/MaxEv100 to keep it in a plausible range
    A scene that stays the same brightness whatever the camera sees ExposureMode.Fixed and an Ev100 (about 15 for sun, 12 overcast, 6 to 8 indoors)
    Photographic control ExposureMode.Camera with aperture, shutter and ISO
    The brightness of the previous renderer for unit-scale content Tonemapper.PbrNeutral, ExposureMode.Fixed, and Ev100 = -log2(1.2) (an exposure of exactly 1)

    The dev overlay's exposure slider is the compensation (RenderSettings.Exposure).

    Tests¶

    • Gt7ToneMappingTests: the curve (toe, linear section, continuity, monotonic, the shoulder's asymptote), PQ against its known values, ICtCp round trips, the gamut matrices against the Rec.709 and Rec.2020 primaries they are derived from, infinities, the shader's literal constants, and the original's outputs for its test inputs.
    • ToneMappingGpuTests: an image of gradients (gray and colors, 0.002 to 30, the last column infinite) goes through tonemap.slang and every pixel is compared with the CPU twin, for GT7 and for Neutral.
    • ExposureMathTests: EV100, the scale, the bins, the percentile average and the adaptation.
    • ExposureTests: the histogram pass against the CPU's bins on an image whose size is not a multiple of the group; the exposure pass against ExposureMath (first frame, brighter, darker, clamped, cleared); an unlit quad covering most of the view not moving the exposure; an emitter of known luminance at several EV100s; the physical camera against the same EV100 set by hand; a lux and a candela giving the same picture with the luminance the BRDF predicts; and auto-exposure following a scene four stops darker with the adaptation's rate and restarting after another mode.
